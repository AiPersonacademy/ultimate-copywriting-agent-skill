<Chapter 14: Media-Specific Copywriting>
> Fuses: Sugarman Ch 35 (Writing for Different Media), Whitman Platform adaptation, Hopkins Ch 19 (Letter Writing) + Ch 15 (Test Campaigns)

## Core Idea
While human psychology remains constant, the execution of copy must adapt to the unique environment, constraints, and consumption habits of the specific medium.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Platform Adaptation Protocol**: Whitman and Sugarman's approach to translating core sales messages across channels.
  - When to use: Taking a winning concept from one medium (e.g., print) to another (e.g., email or TV).
  - How: Retain the core appeal, but adjust pacing, length, and visual elements to match the medium's native format.

- **The Personal Letter Principle**: Hopkins's timeless rules for direct mail and email.
  - When to use: Writing sales letters, autoresponders, or direct messages.
  - How: Write as one human being to another. Use personal pronouns, an intimate tone, and avoid corporate speak.

- **The Micro-Test Campaign**: Hopkins's strategy for mitigating risk in new media.
  - When to use: Rolling out a campaign on a new, unproven platform.
  - How: Spend a tiny fraction of the budget in a representative local market or small segment before scaling.

## Key Concepts
- **Medium Context**: The physical or digital environment in which the ad is consumed (e.g., a quiet living room vs. a noisy subway).
- **Direct Mail/Email Tone**: The conversational, one-on-one style required for letter-based selling.
- **Catalog Copy**: Ultra-dense, feature-heavy copy that relies on the "Sears Roebuck" style of comprehensive listing.
- **Broadcast Pacing**: The necessity in TV/radio to repeat the core offer and call to action multiple times due to fleeting attention.
- **Native Formatting**: Making an ad look and feel like the non-advertising content of the platform it appears on.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the medium as the room**: You speak differently in a boardroom than you do at a cocktail party. Adjust your copy's tone to fit the "room."
- **Use the Letter Principle for all text**: Whether it's direct mail or a digital landing page, imagine you are writing a passionate letter to a single friend.
- **Think of test campaigns as reconnaissance**: Send out a small scout (the micro-test) to report back on enemy territory (the market) before sending in the army (the budget).

## Anti-patterns
- **Copy-Pasting Across Media**: Running a print ad script verbatim as a radio spot. (Why it fails: Radio lacks visual reinforcement; the copy must work harder to paint pictures.)
- **Corporate Tone in Letters**: Using stiff, formal language in direct mail or email. (Why it fails: It destroys the personal connection required for letter-based sales.)
- **Scaling Without Testing**: Launching a national campaign on a new platform without a local test. (Why it fails: Unforeseen medium-specific variables can cause catastrophic failure.)

## Worked Example
*Adapting a single offer (Sugarman):*
*Print/Web:* A long-form sales page exploring the deep technical details and backstory of a new digital watch.
*Catalog:* A 50-word, punchy summary of the watch's top 3 features, focusing heavily on a clear product photo.
*Radio:* A 60-second script relying on sound effects (ticking) and a repetitive, urgent call to action ("Call now to get the watch that survived Everest").

## Key Takeaways
1. Human desires don't change, but how they consume media does; adapt the execution, keep the psychology.
2. When writing letters (or emails), be highly personal. "Me to you" works better than "Us to them."
3. In broadcast (audio/video), repetition of the offer and the URL/phone number is critical.
4. Make your ads look native to the platform—an ad that looks like editorial content gets higher engagement.
5. Always run a cheap, localized test campaign when moving to a completely new advertising medium.
