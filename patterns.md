# Patterns — Ultimate Copywriting Skill

## The Slippery Slide Pattern
**When to use**: Every piece of long-form copy — sales letters, landing pages, emails, ads
**How**: Each element's sole purpose is to get the next element read. Headline → first sentence → second sentence → all the way to the CTA. Remove any sentence that doesn't pull the reader forward.
**Trade-offs**: Requires ruthless editing. Every "interesting aside" that doesn't serve the slide must go.

## The Fear-Resolution Arc
**When to use**: Products that solve problems the reader is already experiencing
**How**: (1) Name the specific fear. (2) Amplify with vivid consequences using sensory language. (3) Present your product as the proven resolution. (4) Show social proof of others who escaped the fear.
**Trade-offs**: Overuse creates "boy who cried wolf" fatigue. The fear must be genuine and the resolution credible.

## Mental Movies Technique
**When to use**: Any copy that needs to create desire for a physical or experiential product
**How**: Replace abstract claims with specific, sensory, visual language. Transform "Go somewhere and do something" → "Walk into your sunlit kitchen, open the oven, and pull out the crispiest, most golden deep-dish pizza you've ever tasted." Each word must install an image on the reader's mental projector screen.
**Trade-offs**: Requires more words. Balance vividness with brevity based on medium.

## The Ego-Morphing Pattern
**When to use**: When the target audience aspires to an identity (fitness, wealth, status, expertise)
**How**: (1) Identify the desired identity. (2) Show someone who already has it using your product. (3) Use "you" language so the reader mentally substitutes themselves. (4) Close with the identity promise: "Be the [aspirational identity] everyone notices."
**Trade-offs**: Falls flat if the aspirational identity doesn't match the actual audience.

## Cure-Not-Prevention Repositioning
**When to use**: Selling a product that is inherently preventative (insurance, supplements, security)
**How**: Reframe from "prevent future problem" to "fix existing pain." Instead of "Protect against burglary" → "Your neighbor was robbed last week. Here's how to make sure you're not next."
**Trade-offs**: Requires an existing pain point or recent event to anchor against.

## The Credibility Stack
**When to use**: Any offer where the reader's default response is skepticism
**How**: Layer multiple proof types in sequence: (1) Extreme specificity ("saves 33.7%, not 'up to 40%'"). (2) Third-party authority. (3) Customer testimonials with names and details. (4) Process transparency (show how it works). (5) Risk reversal (guarantee). Each layer compounds credibility.
**Trade-offs**: Too much proof without emotional pull creates a dry, unpersuasive document.

## Headline-First Architecture
**When to use**: All advertising — print, digital, email, landing pages
**How**: Write 25+ headline candidates before touching body copy. Test each against: (1) Does it state the biggest benefit? (2) Is it specific? (3) Does it select the right audience? (4) Does it create enough curiosity to pull into the first sentence? The headline IS the ad for 60% of readers.
**Trade-offs**: Time-intensive upfront, but highest-ROI activity in the entire copy process.

## Sugarman's Seven-Step Process
**When to use**: Writing any piece of copy from scratch
**How**: (1) Become an expert on the product. (2) Know your prospect's deepest motivations. (3) Write 25+ headline candidates. (4) Write the first draft freely — no editing. (5) Edit ruthlessly for clarity. (6) Incubate — walk away for 24+ hours. (7) Final review with fresh eyes.
**Trade-offs**: Requires patience. The incubation step is non-negotiable for quality.

## Hopkins' Reason-Why Copy
**When to use**: Direct response, mail order, performance marketing
**How**: Give concrete, specific reasons for every claim. Instead of "the best soap" → "We spent $500,000 testing 347 soap formulas over 18 months to find the one that produces the richest lather with the gentlest pH." Every feature gets a "because."
**Trade-offs**: Doesn't work for pure brand/image advertising where emotion dominates.

## The Value-Frame Stack
**When to use**: Justifying price in any offer
**How**: Layer value techniques in sequence: (1) Anchor high ("Worth $2,000"). (2) Stack bonuses ("Plus you get..."). (3) Amortize ("Just $1.25 a day"). (4) Compare ("Less than a cup of coffee"). (5) Guarantee ("Try it free for 30 days"). (6) Use psychological pricing ($997, not $1,000).
**Trade-offs**: Stacking too many value frames feels manipulative. Pick the 2-3 most honest ones.

## The Story Selling Framework
**When to use**: Building emotional connection before the pitch
**How**: (1) Hook with a relatable character facing a recognizable problem. (2) Build tension through specific obstacles. (3) Introduce the "discovery" (your product/solution). (4) Show transformation with specific, measurable results. (5) Invite the reader to begin their own story.
**Trade-offs**: Requires genuine stories. Fabricated stories destroy credibility when discovered.

## The 46-Point Pre-Launch Audit
**When to use**: Before publishing any advertisement
**How**: Run Whitman's complete checklist across Headline (9 checks), Body Copy First Sentence (5 checks), Body Copy General (15 checks), Layout & Design (12 checks), and Response Mechanics (5 checks). See ch18 for the full checklist.
**Trade-offs**: Time-consuming but prevents expensive mistakes. Skip at your own risk.

## Hopkins' Test-and-Measure Pattern
**When to use**: Any campaign where you can measure response
**How**: (1) Create two versions differing in exactly one element. (2) Key each version with unique tracking. (3) Run simultaneously to equivalent audiences. (4) Measure response by key. (5) Keep the winner, test the next variable. (6) Never stop testing.
**Trade-offs**: Requires volume. Not practical for very small audiences or one-shot campaigns.

## The Personalization Pattern
**When to use**: Email, direct mail, landing pages, any one-to-one medium
**How**: (1) Use "you" within the first 10 words. (2) Reference the reader's specific situation/industry/pain. (3) Write as one person talking to one person, never "Dear valued customers." (4) Match the reader's language level. (5) Sign with a real name.
**Trade-offs**: At scale, "personalization" can feel robotic if not genuinely tailored. Quality over quantity.
